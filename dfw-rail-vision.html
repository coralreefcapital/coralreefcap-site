The National Evidence

The Economic Case for Transit Is Settled

Decades of research and the most recent federal data all point the same direction: public transit builds economies. The remaining debate is how boldly to invest.

Why Roads Alone Can't Sustain DFW's Growth

The Dallas–Fort Worth metro added 177,922 people in a single year per the latest Census release — a top-three metro for numeric growth nationally, and the leader as recently as 2022–23. Road capacity cannot keep pace with that growth. Highway expansion induces demand, filling new lanes within years. Rail, by contrast, moves people at scale without proportional land consumption.

The economic cost of congestion in DFW is already measured in billions annually — hours lost, fuel burned, freight delayed, workers priced out of neighborhoods close to jobs. Commuter rail moves people and structures land use around it, and the land-use effect compounds for decades.

Transit as a Property Tax Engine

Every station on a well-designed commuter rail line is a development catalyst. Transit-oriented development (TOD) densifies land use, increases taxable value per acre, and creates walkable commercial districts that generate sales tax. Cities like Denver, Charlotte, and Dallas itself have documented this effect along their existing light rail corridors.

A commuter rail extension to a market like Sherman or Greenville restructures the economic geography of an entire county, bringing workforce mobility and development pressure that highway-only markets attract at a far lower rate.

The 2025 Turning Point: Rail expansion that links Fort Worth's TEXRail with the DART and Denton County systems represents the most significant structural shift in DFW transit in a decade. It creates a single continuous spine, and the extensions to the outer counties should be planned alongside the core, while right-of-way is still open.

The region's existing ~200 miles of rail, including the 26-mile Silver Line that opened in October 2025, serves a metro of 9,000+ square miles and nearly 8 million people. By comparison, Chicago's regional rail network covers 500+ miles; Philadelphia's SEPTA regional rail covers 280 miles for a smaller metro. The gap between DFW's economic scale and its rail coverage is one of the largest in any major American city — and it's growing.

What Each Line Unlocks

Each proposed corridor serves a distinct purpose — from connecting fast-growing exurbs to tapping underserved regional economies that are currently two hours from the metro by car in peak traffic.

North · ~65 mi
Sherman
Grayson County seat · ~145,000 county pop
Texas Instruments chip fab expansion makes Grayson County a semiconductor corridor. Rail would allow workers to commute bidirectionally — Sherman residents into DFW, DFW talent into Sherman plants.
North-Northwest · ~80 mi
Gainesville
Cooke County seat · I-35 corridor
Positioned on a major NAFTA-era freight corridor. A commuter line on this route would also serve Denton and the tech cluster emerging along I-35. Gainesville is a gateway to Oklahoma and underserved by transit entirely.
Northwest · ~40 mi
Decatur
Wise County seat · Rural workforce hub
Wise County commuters already flood US-287 into Fort Worth daily. Rail would reduce a grinding commute, support local hiring by county-seat employers, and enable Decatur to retain workers who currently relocate closer to Fort Worth.
West · ~30 mi
Weatherford
Parker County seat · Fastest-growing segment
Parker County is one of the fastest-growing counties in Texas. Weatherford is the nearest major city to Fort Worth without rail access. This corridor is the most immediately viable — short distance, dense commute pattern, existing I-20 freight ROW.
Southwest · ~42 mi
Granbury
Hood County seat · Tourism + retirement economy
A growing retirement and tourism destination that sends workers into Fort Worth daily. Rail would support a two-way economy — residents commuting out, visitors arriving in. Hood County's lack of transit is a persistent equity gap.
South-Southwest · ~38 mi
Cleburne
Johnson County seat · Manufacturing base
Cleburne has a significant manufacturing and logistics workforce that commutes north into Fort Worth and Arlington. It also hosts Tarleton State University. Rail access would be transformative for workforce mobility and college enrollment.
Southeast · ~75 mi
Athens
Henderson County seat · Trinity River basin
An Athens–Tyler corridor would serve the most underconnected quadrant of the DFW region. Henderson County has limited economic mobility infrastructure. A rail line would also serve intermediate stops like Mabank and Canton.
East · ~95 mi
Tyler
Smith County · Largest East Texas metro
Tyler is the economic capital of East Texas — a city of 115,000 with a major medical complex, university, and commercial hub. A rail link would functionally integrate East Texas into the DFW economy for the first time, unlocking enormous latent labor mobility.
Northeast · ~48 mi
Greenville
Hunt County seat · I-30 corridor
Hunt County is already experiencing DFW suburban sprawl pressure. Rail before full suburban buildout preserves the chance to shape density around stations rather than retrofit it later. Greenville's Hunt Regional Medical Center is a key employment anchor.
The Cost of Waiting

There Is Already a Ghost Station Under Knox Street

Dallas has run this experiment once. The result is sitting eighty feet under one of the most valuable retail districts in Texas, sealed behind a block wall.

DART's original 1985 tunnel design for the Central corridor included two underground stations: Cityplace and Knox-Henderson. Residents of Vickery Place, the neighborhood just east, opposed the Knox station, and DART removed it from the plan in the early 1990s. During tunnel construction the agency spent roughly $1 million excavating the station cavity anyway: a 400-foot platform area, a ventilation shaft, and a stairwell. It was sealed and serves today as an emergency exit.

Within a few years the same neighborhood petitioned DART to build the station after all. By then there was no funding and no priority. A 2006 estimate put the cost of finishing the station at roughly $100 million, most of it driven by the constraint of working midnight to 4 a.m. around live trains. The station has been excluded from every long-range plan since, including the 2030 and 2045 Transit System Plans.

Knox-Henderson went on to become one of the strongest districts in the region without the station: office rents that lead the Sunbelt outside Miami per JLL, triple-digit retail rents, and a development pipeline that now includes a million-plus square feet anchored by a 27-story tower over the district, directly above the sealed shell.

The provision cost $1 million. The retrofit was quoted at $100 million. The neighborhood that blocked the station asked for it back within a few years, and the district became exactly the kind of place a station serves. The trains pass under it without stopping. Securing right-of-way and station provisions for the nine corridors above, now, while they are cheap, is how the region avoids repeating this.

The Anchor Institution Argument

Relocate Smaller Colleges to City Centers

Transit and education are the same policy question. A commuter rail network gains its full value when the destinations it connects include dense employment, retail, and institutional anchors. Community colleges and small four-year schools are among the highest-value anchors available.

What Research Shows About Anchor Institutions

The University of Pennsylvania's Institute for Urban Research defines anchor institutions as place-based organizations — hospitals, universities, colleges — that are unlikely to relocate and generate sustained economic activity in their surrounding neighborhoods. When these institutions are in or near urban cores, the multiplier effects are dramatically higher.

Research published through Penn IUR and the Philadelphia Federal Reserve shows that anchors create supply chains of local purchasing, employ lower-income workers in accessible service roles, and support spin-off businesses at measurable rates within a two-mile radius.

A July 2025 Urban Institute report on community colleges contributing value to communities found that proximity to transit and employers directly correlates with better student outcomes — higher completion rates, better employment placement, and higher wages after graduation.

The Specific Case for DFW

The DFW region has significant higher education assets that are marooned on suburban campuses designed for an era of universal car ownership. Several community college districts — including Dallas College and Tarrant County College — have already begun building downtown and near-downtown footprints. The question is whether smaller institutions follow.

A community college campus within walking distance of a commuter rail station doesn't require students to own a car to access education. It doesn't require faculty to commute in from fifty miles away on congested highways. It activates daytime street life in urban cores, supports adjacent retail, and creates a visible presence that signals economic vitality to employers considering relocation.

Stockton University's Atlantic City campus is a documented case study: a university anchor shifted local economic trajectory, supporting retail, hospitality, and housing investment in a corridor that had stagnated for two decades. The institution and the neighborhood feed each other.

01

Workforce Pipeline at Scale

Urban community colleges place graduates directly into adjacent labor markets. Students who complete degrees without a car get jobs they can reach without a car. Transit access closes the loop between education and employment in a way suburban campuses cannot.

02

Downtown Activation

College campuses in city centers generate 10–14 hours of active daytime use across retail, food service, and public space. This is the single most reliable way to support urban commercial districts outside of office development — and it's more resilient to remote work trends.

03

Enrollment Access Equity

Low-income students are significantly more likely to complete programs when they can reach campus via transit rather than depending on car access. Relocating campuses to transit-served locations is a direct equity intervention.

04

Land Value Creation

A college campus in a transit-accessible urban location commands premium real estate value for surrounding parcels within 5 years of opening. For smaller cities along proposed commuter corridors — Cleburne, Weatherford, Greenville — a downtown campus is a station-area catalyst.

The Case for Action

What North Texas Could Be

The economic research argues for network thinking, because the returns from transit accrue to the whole network. A single spoke to Weatherford is useful. Nine spokes connecting a unified regional system is transformational.

Build the Network First

Corridors to Weatherford and Cleburne are the shortest and most immediately viable. Start there — but plan the full nine-corridor system now so right-of-way is preserved and future construction doesn't require politically costly land acquisition.

Zone for Transit Before the Train Arrives

The best time to shape land use around future stations is before the rail arrives. Station-area zoning in Greenville, Athens, and Granbury today sets the conditions for TOD development that compounds in value for thirty years.

Move the Colleges Toward the Tracks

Tarleton State in Stephenville, Texas A&M Commerce, Navarro College, and others all have campuses far from transit. Branch campuses in Cleburne, Greenville, or along future rail corridors should be a condition of new state higher education funding.

Capture the Land Value

Implement tax increment financing (TIF) districts along proposed corridors now. Even before rail arrives, TIF-backed investment in walkable commercial development at future station sites creates the density that makes rail economically viable when it opens.

Make the Case in Austin

Texas Rail Advocates petitioned TxDOT to participate in the FRA Corridor ID Program. That federal process is the mechanism for funding. The DFW region needs a unified, loudly articulated vision — not four separate transit authorities lobbying in separate lanes.

Think Beyond the Commute

Commuter rail done right serves the student in Cleburne attending Tarrant County College. The nurse in Weatherford whose hospital is in Fort Worth. The family in Greenville that can't afford DFW rents but could afford a train ticket.
